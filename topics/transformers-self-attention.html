1. The Transformer
Intuition

Before the Transformer, sequence models processed tokens one at a time, left to right. A recurrent network (RNN or LSTM) maintained a "hidden state" that was updated at each step — a kind of running memory. The problem: to connect the first word of a sentence to the last, information had to travel through every intermediate step. Long sequences caused it to fade or distort.

The Transformer, introduced by Vaswani et al. in 2017,[1] removes recurrence entirely. Its central idea is that every position in a sequence should be able to communicate directly with every other position in a single operation. There is no step-by-step propagation; the whole sequence is processed at once.

This has two important consequences. First, long-range dependencies are no longer harder than short-range ones — the first word can attend directly to the last word with the same mechanism. Second, because there is no sequential dependency between steps, the entire encoder can run in parallel across all positions, which makes training on modern hardware drastically faster.

The Transformer has an encoder (which reads the input) and a decoder (which generates the output). Each is a stack of identical layers. Every encoder layer does two things: it lets each position attend to all others (self-attention), then it applies a small feed-forward network to each position independently.

Formal

The Transformer encoder consists of \(N\) identical layers stacked in sequence. In the original paper,[1] \(N = 6\) and the model dimension is \(d_{\text{model}} = 512\).

Encoder layer

Each encoder layer applies two sublayers. Let \(\mathbf{x}\) be the input to a sublayer. Each sublayer wraps its operation with a residual connection and layer normalization:[1]

\[ \text{output} = \text{LayerNorm}\!\left(\mathbf{x} + \text{Sublayer}(\mathbf{x})\right) \]

Sublayer 1 is multi-head self-attention (covered in Concepts 2 and 3).
Sublayer 2 is a position-wise feed-forward network:

\[ \text{FFN}(\mathbf{x}) = \max(0,\, \mathbf{x}\mathbf{W}_1 + \mathbf{b}_1)\,\mathbf{W}_2 + \mathbf{b}_2 \]

where \(\mathbf{W}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{ff}}}\), \(\mathbf{W}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{ff}} \times d_{\text{model}}}\), and \(d_{\text{ff}} = 2048\) in the original model.[1] This FFN is applied identically and independently to each position — there is no communication between positions in this sublayer.

Decoder layer

The decoder layer adds a third sublayer between the two above: a cross-attention mechanism that attends over the encoder's output (rather than the decoder's own sequence). The decoder also masks future positions in its self-attention to preserve the autoregressive property during training.[1]

Notation note. Throughout this page, matrices are bold uppercase (\(\mathbf{W}\), \(\mathbf{Q}\)), vectors bold lowercase (\(\mathbf{x}\)), and scalars italic (\(d_k\)), consistent with Vaswani et al. (2017).[1]
Applied

Modern LLMs use decoder-only Transformers (GPT-2, GPT-3, GPT-4, Llama, Claude) — the encoder half is dropped, and generation is autoregressive. BERT and its derivatives use encoder-only Transformers for classification and representation learning.

The elimination of recurrence unlocked training on datasets and compute scales that were impractical for RNNs. Parallelism across sequence positions maps directly onto GPU tensor cores, which is why the Transformer scaling laws held so well.

What this connects to later in the course: because the Transformer has no built-in notion of position (all positions are processed in parallel and symmetrically), positional encoding must be added explicitly to the input embeddings. Without it, the model cannot distinguish "cat sat on mat" from "mat on sat cat." This is covered in the Positional Encoding topic.

2. Self-Attention
Intuition

Consider the sentence "The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired."[2] What does "it" refer to — the animal or the street? Humans resolve this immediately, but for a model that processes words one at a time, the pronoun appears long after the relevant noun. Self-attention is the mechanism that lets the model bring those two positions into direct contact.

For each position in the sequence, self-attention asks: which other positions are relevant to understanding me? The answer is not binary — it is a weighted average of all positions, where the weights reflect relevance. A position can draw on many others at once, with each contribution scaled by how relevant it is.

The Query–Key–Value analogy

The self-attention mechanism is built around three learned projections of each token's embedding,[1][2] which can be understood as three roles:

Query (Q) — "What information am I looking for from others?" Each token formulates a query representing its information need.

Key (K) — "What information do I have to offer?" Each token broadcasts a key representing what it contains.

Value (V) — "What content do I actually share when selected?" Each token holds a value that gets passed along weighted by how well its key matched another token's query.

Computing attention is like a soft lookup: instead of retrieving exactly one item from a table, you get a weighted average of all items, with weights determined by how well your query matches each key. The "soft" part is crucial — gradients flow through all positions, so the model can learn which queries and keys to build.

Formal

Let \(\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_{\text{model}}}\) be the input sequence of \(n\) token embeddings. Compute three linear projections:[1]

\[ \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^Q, \quad \mathbf{K} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^K, \quad \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^V \]

where \(\mathbf{W}^Q, \mathbf{W}^K \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_k}\) and \(\mathbf{W}^V \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_v}\) are learned weight matrices.

Scaled dot-product attention

The attention output is:[1]

\[ \text{Attention}(\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\mathbf{V} \]

Step by step:

1. \(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) — the raw attention scores matrix. Entry \((i, j)\) is the dot product of the query at position \(i\) with the key at position \(j\), measuring their compatibility.

2. Divide by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) — the scaling factor. Vaswani et al. note that for large \(d_k\), the dot products grow large in magnitude, pushing the softmax into regions with extremely small gradients.[1] Dividing by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) counteracts this. Weng (2018) frames this as keeping the pre-softmax scores in a variance-stable regime.[5]

3. \(\text{softmax}(\cdot)\) applied row-wise — each row of the result sums to 1, giving a probability distribution over positions for each query.

4. Multiply by \(\mathbf{V}\) — for each query position, compute a weighted average of all value vectors, with the weights given by the attention distribution.

Computational complexity

The matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top\) has size \(n \times n\), giving \(\mathcal{O}(n^2 d_k)\) time and \(\mathcal{O}(n^2)\) space per layer. This quadratic dependence on sequence length \(n\) is the central computational bottleneck of Transformers.[1]

Applied

In GPT-2 (small),[6] \(d_{\text{model}} = 768\), with 12 attention heads giving \(d_k = 64\) per head, and a maximum sequence length of 1024 tokens. The attention matrix is \(1024 \times 1024\) per layer per head.

The \(\mathcal{O}(n^2)\) memory cost of the attention matrix is why long contexts are expensive. A 128 000-token context window produces attention matrices with \(128{,}000^2 \approx 16\) billion entries per layer per head — before considering the float storage. This is the direct motivation for KV-cache and PagedAttention, covered later in the course: during autoregressive generation, keys and values for previously seen tokens can be cached and reused rather than recomputed.

Each embedding is linearly projected into three 4-dimensional spaces (dk = dv = 4). Q = X·WQ ("what am I looking for?"), K = X·WK ("what do I offer?"), V = X·WV ("what content do I share?").

Entry (i, j) is the dot product of query i with key j — how much token i's question matches token j's content. Range for this head: . Raw values can be large in magnitude; that is exactly why we need the next step.

Step 4 Scaled scores QKT / T ← changes with T

Dividing by T compresses the score range. Too small a T leaves extreme values that push softmax toward one-hot attention; too large a T collapses everything toward zero and makes softmax uniform. The default T = √dk = 2 keeps the pre-softmax variance stable.[1]

Row-wise softmax converts scaled scores to a probability distribution. Each row sums to 1. Darker cells = higher weight. Row i shows how much token i attends to each other token.

Step 6 Output weights · V ← changes with T

Each token's output is a weighted average of all value vectors, using the weights from step 5. This context-aware representation replaces the original embedding and flows to the next sub-layer.

3. Multi-Head Attention
Intuition

A single attention head produces one "reading" of the sequence — one weighted combination of positions based on one set of learned query, key, and value projections. But a sequence contains multiple, simultaneous types of relationships. Consider "The cat sat on the mat":

— Syntactic: "cat" is the subject of "sat"
— Positional: "sat" is adjacent to "on"
— Semantic: "mat" and "cat" both refer to objects in a domestic scene

A single head can only attend according to one learned criterion at a time. To capture multiple relationship types simultaneously, the Transformer runs \(h\) parallel attention heads, each with its own independent weight matrices.[1] The outputs are then concatenated and projected back to \(d_{\text{model}}\).

Think of it as \(h\) different readers examining the same text, each trained to look for something different, then pooling their observations. Empirical analysis of trained Transformers confirms that heads do specialize: some heads track syntactic dependencies, others track coreference, others positional proximity.[3][4]

Formal

For \(h\) attention heads, each head \(i\) has its own weight matrices \(\mathbf{W}_i^Q \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_k}\), \(\mathbf{W}_i^K \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_k}\), \(\mathbf{W}_i^V \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_v}\), and there is a shared output projection \(\mathbf{W}^O \in \mathbb{R}^{h d_v \times d_{\text{model}}}\). Each head computes:[1]

\[ \text{head}_i = \text{Attention}\!\left(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{W}_i^Q,\; \mathbf{K}\mathbf{W}_i^K,\; \mathbf{V}\mathbf{W}_i^V\right) \]

The multi-head output is:

\[ \text{MultiHead}(\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}) = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_1, \ldots, \text{head}_h)\,\mathbf{W}^O \]

In self-attention, \(\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{K} = \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{X}\) (the sequence attends to itself).

Dimension bookkeeping

To keep the total computation comparable to single-head attention with \(d_{\text{model}}\)-dimensional Q and K, Vaswani et al. set \(d_k = d_v = d_{\text{model}} / h\).[1] In the original paper: \(h = 8\), \(d_{\text{model}} = 512\), \(d_k = d_v = 64\).

Each \(\text{head}_i\) output is \(\in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_v}\). The concatenation is \(\in \mathbb{R}^{n \times h d_v} = \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_{\text{model}}}\). Multiplying by \(\mathbf{W}^O \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{model}}}\) produces the final output \(\in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_{\text{model}}}\), matching the input shape for the residual connection.

Parameter count

The \(h\) matrices \(\mathbf{W}_i^Q\) together have \(h \cdot d_{\text{model}} \cdot d_k = h \cdot d_{\text{model}} \cdot (d_{\text{model}}/h) = d_{\text{model}}^2\) parameters. Likewise for \(\mathbf{W}_i^K\), \(\mathbf{W}_i^V\), and \(\mathbf{W}^O\). Total parameters in the multi-head attention block: \(4 d_{\text{model}}^2\), independent of \(h\).[1]

Applied

Clark et al. (2019)[4] analyzed BERT's attention heads and found clear specialization: certain heads attend to direct objects of verbs, others to coreferent mentions, others to the separator [SEP] token almost exclusively. Vig & Belinkov (2019)[3] confirmed that syntactic structure is encoded in specific heads.

This head specialization is one entry point into mechanistic interpretability — the attempt to reverse-engineer what specific components of a neural network compute. The "attention head as a feature detector" framing is revisited in the Mechanistic Interpretability topic.

Modern scale: GPT-2 (small) uses 12 heads with \(d_{\text{model}} = 768\). GPT-3 (175B) uses 96 heads with \(d_{\text{model}} = 12{,}288\).[6][7] The number of heads scales with model size, and each head at larger scale has more capacity (\(d_k = 128\) in GPT-3 vs. 64 in the original Transformer).
