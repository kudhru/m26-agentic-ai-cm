The Living Textbook A proposal · 2026
Rethinking Learning Materials

Books didn't become digital.
They just became PDFs.

The move from print to screen changed the medium but left the pedagogy untouched. AI gives us the first real opportunity to rethink what a learning resource can be — from the ground up.

The Problem

The assumptions we inherited from paper

Every CS textbook today — print or digital — carries the same structural assumptions. These were never pedagogical choices. They were constraints imposed by the physical medium of paper that survived the transition to digital unchanged.

Assumption 01

One linear reading order for all readers, regardless of background or goal.

Assumption 02

One explanation per concept, written for an imagined average reader.

Assumption 03

Static code examples — screenshots of code you can't run or modify.

Assumption 04

A single difficulty level throughout, fixed at the time of writing.

Assumption 05

Passive consumption — the reader receives, the book does not respond.

Assumption 06

A snapshot in time — published once, relevant for a window, then stale.

None of these are necessary. They are artifacts of paper constraints that survived digitization because no one questioned them.

The Framework

Two distinct problems, often conflated

Most attempts to modernize learning materials fail because they try to solve two fundamentally different problems at once. Separating them is the first step toward solving either.

Lens 01 — Production

AI as a building tool

Using AI to produce richer, more varied, and more current learning materials than were previously economically possible. The output is still something you publish and verify. A human author is always in the loop.

Lens 02 — Runtime

AI as a learning participant

Integrating AI into the experience of learning itself — adapting, explaining, questioning, personalising. This is a service, not a document. It requires ongoing compute, feedback loops, and a different product model.

These have different owners, different timelines, and different failure modes. Lens 1 produces a richer artifact. Lens 2 produces a responsive system. This document focuses entirely on Lens 1 — because it is the more tractable problem, and because getting the artifact right is a prerequisite for everything that comes after.

The Model

What the Living Textbook is built on

Seven structural principles that distinguish this approach from a PDF, an LMS course, or an interactive tutorial.

01

Concept nodes, not chapters

The atomic unit is a self-contained concept — with its own prerequisites, explanations, code, visuals, and exercises — not an author's organisational chapter. The book becomes a graph you navigate, not a sequence you follow.

02

Layered explanations as standard

Every concept ships with multiple entry points: intuition, formal, code, and applied. Not a chatbot — just designed that way from the start. The reader chooses depth; the material accommodates it.

03

Runnable code as a first-class citizen

Code is not a screenshot or a link to an external environment. It lives inside the material, is executable, annotatable, and modifiable. Reading and doing happen in the same place.

04

Visual-first for visual concepts

Things that are inherently spatial or dynamic — memory layout, network flow, algorithm steps — are designed visually first, with prose as the supplement, not the primary medium.

05

Structured, tagged exercise banks

Problems are woven through the material, tagged by concept and difficulty, and abundant enough to serve both self-study and structured courses. AI makes the generation cost negligible.

06

Modular and updateable

Because the unit is a node, any concept can be updated, corrected, or replaced without a new edition. The book ages like a codebase, not a print run.

07

Explorable by default

Any concept with a parameter, a state, or a sequence is designed for manipulation — not just description. The reader does not observe; they perturb, break, and discover. "What if I change this?" is the question a textbook can never answer. An explorable always can. This changes the relationship between reader and material from audience to experimenter.

What Changes

How AI collapses the cost of content richness

The bottlenecks that made rich learning materials expensive were never about knowledge. They were about production. AI removes most of them.

Old Bottleneck What AI changes Author's new role
Diagrams & visuals Generatable, iterable, consistent style Art direction, accuracy review
Multiple explanation styles Draft all variants in minutes Select, refine, sequence
Exercise banks Generate hundreds with difficulty tags Sample, verify, curate
Worked examples Draft at scale, including edge cases Verify correctness, add insight
Keeping content current Flag stale sections, suggest rewrites Approve or reject updates
Localization Quality translation at near-zero cost Domain review in target language

The author's role shifts from writing everything to curating, verifying, and structuring — which is what the best authors were always best at.

Applied Example

A Living Textbook for
Large Language Models & AI Agents

Why this topic demands the new format — and what it would actually look like in practice.

Why This Topic

LLMs and AI Agents are uniquely ill-served by traditional formats

The field moves weekly

A printed textbook on LLMs would be outdated before the ink dried. Modular, updateable nodes are not a nice-to-have — they are essential.

Concepts are inherently visual

Attention mechanisms, token distributions, agent loops, RAG pipelines — all painful in prose, obvious with the right diagram.

Code must run

Reading about how to call an LLM API or build a tool-calling agent without running it is nearly meaningless. The gap between reading and doing is huge.

Multiple valid entry points

Students arrive as engineers, ML researchers, product managers, or complete beginners. One linear path serves none of them well.

Structure

The knowledge graph for the course

Rather than chapters, the course is structured as a graph of concept nodes. Each node is self-contained, prerequisite-linked, and independently updateable.

A student who already understands transformers can enter at "LLM Behaviour". A researcher wanting only agents can skip directly there, prerequisites surfaced automatically. A teacher can pull any subset and sequence it for their syllabus.

Node Anatomy

What a single concept node looks like

Take Attention Mechanism. In a traditional textbook this is one section at one depth. In a Living Textbook node, it has four layers — all present, all human-authored, all accessible from the same entry point.

Intuition layer
"When the model processes each word, it learns to look at other words that are relevant to its meaning. Attention is the mechanism that decides how much weight to give each other word."
Formal layer
The Query, Key, Value matrix formulation. Scaled dot-product attention. Softmax normalisation. Multi-head variants. The actual mathematics with notation consistent with the original Vaswani et al. paper.
Code layer
A minimal, runnable PyTorch implementation. An annotated version explaining every line. A "break it" variant — what happens when you remove the scaling factor. A challenge: extend to multi-head.
Applied layer
Visualisations of real attention patterns in a live model. How this manifests as "in-context learning". Where attention breaks down and why. Links to nodes on context windows and KV cache.
Prerequisites: Linear algebra · Transformers overview Leads to: Multi-head attention · KV cache · Positional encoding
Implementation

How to build it — four phases

The hardest part is not the technology. It is the upfront structural work. An instructor who already teaches this course has the critical advantage: the knowledge graph already exists in their head.

  1. Phase 01
    Map the knowledge graph

    Enumerate every concept in the course. Define prerequisites and downstream connections. This becomes the master architecture — the skeleton everything else attaches to.

  2. Phase 02
    Design the node template

    Define what every node must contain: which layers are mandatory, how code is embedded, how visuals are specified, how exercises are tagged. Consistency here is what makes the graph navigable.

  3. Phase 03
    Author and verify the nodes

    AI drafts at each layer. The author reviews for accuracy, depth, and insight — the parts that require genuine domain knowledge. Code is run and tested. Visuals are checked for correctness.

  4. Phase 04
    Choose a rendering surface

    The graph renders as a web-native document — not a PDF, not an LMS. A PDF export exists as a degraded fallback. The primary experience is interactive, structured, and lives in a browser.

The Core Shift

From document to knowledge graph

The deepest change is not the format or the interactivity. It is the unit. A chapter is an author's organisational choice. A concept node is a piece of understanding — self-contained, prerequisite-linked, and composable into any path a reader or teacher needs. When that becomes the atomic unit, the "book" becomes a database of understanding that renders differently for every reader, every use case, and every update cycle.

A framework for rethinking CS learning materials in the age of AI. Applied here to a course on Large Language Models and AI Agents — a subject that, more than any other, demands the format it describes.
